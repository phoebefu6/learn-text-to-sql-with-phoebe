Part 0

Why governance is where pilots die - or deserve to

An LLM in the query path changes three things your existing BI governance never had to handle: a component that can produce a plausible wrong answer, a query author that is not a person, and answers that can differ between runs. None of these are reasons to say no. All of them are reasons to ask better questions than "what accuracy does the vendor claim" - and this session gives you those questions.

Part 1 · covers Genie/Cortex/Vanna governance facts

Access control with an LLM in the loop 12 min live

The first question for any text-to-SQL tool is not about AI at all: when the system runs a query, WHO does it run as? If the answer is "the asking user, under their existing permissions," your row-level security keeps working. If the answer is "a service account" or "whatever data got exported," your access model just silently dissolved.

Self-studyAudit trails: the last-Tuesday test2 min read▶

A finance-grade audit question: can you reproduce why the system answered X last Tuesday? That requires logging the question, the exact SQL generated, the context that was retrieved to generate it, and the result returned - because a regenerated answer may differ. If the tool logs only "user asked a question," you cannot investigate a bad number after the fact, and every silent failure becomes unexplainable. Ask for the replay capability in the procurement conversation, not after the incident.

Part 2 · covers Genie nondeterminism, show-the-SQL pattern

Trust calibration: the plausible wrong answer 10 min live

Recall session 2's core distinction: loud failures error out and announce themselves; silent failures return a plausible wrong number that ships into a real decision. That silent failure is the number one risk of this whole product category - and the mitigations are organizational as much as technical.

LiveThree facts that set the trust posture5 min▶
  • Same question, different SQL. Databricks documents Genie as nondeterministic: the same question can yield different SQL on different runs. That is not a scandal - it is how LLMs work - but it means "we tested it once" is not a test, and it is why audit logs must capture the SQL actually run.
  • Multi-candidate + human-visible SQL is the working mitigation. Generating several candidate queries and scoring them (the engineering pattern from Salesforce's production system) reduces wrong answers; showing the winning SQL lets a human catch what scoring missed.
  • Show-the-SQL is a trust feature, not a technical detail. Readable SQL means an auditable answer - your analysts become the reviewers of the machine's work instead of being replaced by it. A tool that hides its SQL is asking your organization to trust an unreviewable black box with numbers that reach decisions.
The posture to set from the top Treat every answer as a draft from a fast junior analyst: excellent throughput, zero accountability. Drafts that inform real decisions get reviewed - which means the tool must make review possible (visible SQL) and someone must own doing it (an escalation path for suspected wrong answers).
Part 3 · covers CIDR 2026 benchmark audit

Benchmark skepticism as a governance skill 8 min live

Session 3 warned that vendor accuracy claims are internal and non-comparable. This session goes further, with evidence: even the academic benchmarks the whole field cites turn out to be substantially wrong - so any accuracy claim, from anyone, deserves audit-grade skepticism.

LiveThe governance conclusion: bake-offs on YOUR data only4 min▶

Chain the evidence: vendor benchmarks are internal and non-comparable (both flagship vendors claim 90%+); academic benchmarks carry 52.8-66.1% annotation errors; and correcting the labels reshuffled the rankings. The only number with governance standing is one you can audit end to end:

  • Accept only bake-offs on your golden set, with your data, scored by execution accuracy - the same questions, the same warehouse, every vendor. Session 6 shows how to build that set in a week.
  • Put it in procurement language: "accuracy claims must be reproduced on customer-provided evaluation questions before contract" is a sentence your procurement team already knows how to enforce.
  • Keep the golden set private. A test the vendor has seen is a test the vendor can curate for - the same lesson session 4 taught about quality being local to curation.
Real world

The audit that reshuffled a leaderboard. A UIUC team audited the field's two flagship benchmarks for CIDR 2026 and hand-checked the gold answers: 52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev and 66.1% of Spider 2.0-Snow answers were wrong, mostly gold queries that did not match the actual data. On the corrected subset, CHESS - a system ranked 4th - jumped from 62% to 81% and took 1st place. Every SOTA claim you had read about those systems compared them against a broken answer key. That is why "we top the benchmark" should move you exactly zero dollars.

Demo · the checklist

Governance review walk-through: a hypothetical Genie pilot ★ 10 min · live on screen

Your sales analytics team wants to pilot Databricks Genie on the revenue schema. Before anyone says yes, walk this five-point review - out loud, in this order. It works unchanged for Cortex, an open-source deployment, or any vendor in session 3's landscape.

Identity. Ask: who does the generated query run as? For Genie the answer should be the asking user via Unity Catalog. Get it demonstrated, not asserted - one query, one user, show me the audit log line.

RLS inheritance. Have two users with different row-level permissions ask the identical revenue question. If they get identical numbers, RLS is not being inherited - stop the pilot until it is.

Logging and replay. Pick yesterday's answer and ask the team to reproduce it: question, generated SQL, retrieved context, result. If they cannot replay it, you cannot investigate a wrong number after the fact - and Genie is documented as nondeterministic, so the log is the only record of what actually ran.

Wrong-answer escalation. Who does a business user tell when a number looks off, and what happens next? Name a person, define a response, and make show-the-SQL part of the review - your analysts are the audit function here.

Benchmark demanded. Require the pilot to be scored on your own golden set before expansion - not the vendor's internal benchmark. Set the bar and the silent-failure tolerance in writing now, so the expansion decision in session 6 is a measurement, not a negotiation.

Why this order The first three are gates - fail any one and the pilot is not safe at any accuracy. The last two are calibration - they make the pilot's results mean something. Most failed deployments skipped straight to accuracy and never asked the first three.
Homework

Five questions for your team this week ◐ 20 min total

  • Ask your platform owner: for any AI-over-data tool we run or evaluate, who does the query run as? If the answer takes more than a day to establish, that is itself the finding.
  • Ask security: do we have people exporting warehouse data to external AI tools today? The shadow path in this session exists at most companies before any official pilot does.
  • Ask the data team to run the last-Tuesday test on any current BI or AI tool: reproduce one answer from last week - question, query, result. Time how long it takes.
  • Ask who a business user would tell today if an AI-generated number looked wrong - and watch how long the room stays silent. That silence is your escalation-path gap.
  • Ask procurement to add one sentence to the next data-tool RFP: accuracy claims must be reproduced on customer-provided evaluation questions. Report back what vendors say.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why are silent failures a governance problem, not just an accuracy problem?

Governance exists to make failures visible, attributable, and correctable. Silent failures defeat all three unless you mandate audit logs, show-the-SQL review, and a wrong-answer escalation path - which is why they lead the checklist.

2 · Why do vendor benchmark claims fail audit standards?

No fabrication needed: internal benchmarks measure different questions on different data, so 90% and 90% are not comparable. And the CIDR 2026 audit showed even the field's flagship answer keys were majorly wrong. The only audit-grade number is a bake-off on your golden set with your data.

3 · What is the FIRST access-control question to ask any text-to-SQL tool?

This one question sorts the market: Cortex and Genie keep queries inside existing warehouse governance, Vanna v2 added a user-aware identity flow + RLS, and CSV-export tools bypass governance entirely. Everything else in the checklist depends on this answer.
