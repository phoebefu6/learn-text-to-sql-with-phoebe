Part 1 · covers Genie curation guidance, the accuracy ladder

Scope small, measure like the pros 9 min live

The number-one adoption failure is scope. Teams point the tool at the whole warehouse so "users can ask anything," accuracy craters, trust dies in week two, and the project is quietly shelved. The fix is unglamorous and reliable: start tiny.

LiveThe weekly number that keeps everyone honest4 min▶

Pick one metric and report it every week: execution accuracy on your golden set - the share of questions where the tool's answer matches the human-verified answer. Not "users seem happy," not "the demo went well." A hard number on real questions, tracked over time, is what separates a real rollout from a hopeful one.

  • The ladder is your roadmap conversation. When accuracy is low, the fix is a known sequence: better schema context, then worked examples, then retrieved metadata, then metric definitions, then a retry loop. That is the builder track's measured climb (8% to 100%), and it is the language to use with your data team.
  • Track silent failures separately. A plausible wrong answer is worse than an error - count them by hand each week. Zero tolerance for silent failures on high-stakes questions is a reasonable gate.
  • Engineering, not model-swapping, moves the number. Salesforce's production agent launched at roughly 50% and reached about 80% - not by changing models, but by generating 10 candidate queries and scoring them (a vendor-reported case). Expect the same: the wins come from context and process.
Real world

The rollout that survived. A data leader scoped a pilot to five finance tables and twenty golden questions, reported one accuracy number in the Monday standup, and refused to expand until it cleared 90% with zero silent failures on the revenue questions. It took six weeks and looked slow. The parallel team that opened the whole warehouse "for feedback" hit 30%, lost the executive sponsor by week three, and never recovered. Small and measured beat big and hopeful.

Part 2 · synthesis of a1-a5

Buy, build, or wait 7 min live

Not every organization should move now. The honest framework has three doors, and "wait" is often the right one - because a text-to-SQL tool pointed at undefined metrics just automates wrong answers faster.

DoorChoose it whenWhat it commits you to
BuyYour data already lives in Databricks or Snowflake, and you want standard self-serve for a defined domainThe native tool (Genie / Cortex) plus the curation hours to feed it - the tool is cheap, the semantic model is the work
BuildYou need to embed chat-with-data into your own product, or have governance needs no vendor meetsAn engineering team owning the pipeline you saw in the builder track - and its maintenance forever
WaitNo metric definitions are written down anywhere; nobody owns "what counts as revenue"Paying the curation tax first - define your metrics before any tool can be trusted with them
The "wait" door is not defeat If your organization cannot answer "what counts as an active customer" in one agreed sentence, no text-to-SQL tool will either - it will just guess, plausibly and wrongly. The highest-return move is often to write the semantic definitions first (a4's curation tax), which pays off for every dashboard and analyst too, not only the AI tool.
Guided exercise

Draft the one-page pilot charter ★ 10 min · take it to Monday

Fill these five blanks and you have a charter that would pass any steering committee. Do it now, on a real domain in your organization.

Scope: which one domain and which 5 tables? (Name them. If you cannot name 5, you have not scoped tightly enough.)

Golden set owner: who writes and maintains the 20-50 questions with verified SQL? One accountable name, not a committee.

Weekly metric: execution accuracy on the golden set, plus silent-failure count. Reported where and to whom?

Kill criteria: what number, unmet by what date, ends the pilot honestly? (Deciding this up front prevents zombie projects.)

Expansion gate: what accuracy and silent-failure threshold must hold before adding the next domain or tables?

★ The charter is the deliverable This one page is worth more than any tool trial. It forces the two decisions that actually determine success - tight scope and honest measurement - before a dollar is spent. A team with this charter and no tool yet is further ahead than a team with a tool and no charter.
Homework

Questions to bring to your team ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Can we name exactly 5 tables and one domain for a first pilot? If the list keeps growing, we have not scoped it - cut it down.
  • Who owns our golden set? If the answer is "nobody yet," that hire or assignment is step one, before any vendor conversation.
  • Write our top 3 business metrics in one agreed sentence each (revenue, active customer, churn). If we cannot, we are behind the "wait" door - and that is the finding.
  • Which door are we behind - buy, build, or wait - and what is the single fact that decides it (where our data lives, or whether our metrics are defined)?
  • Draft the five-line charter from the exercise for one real domain, and circulate it before we evaluate a single tool.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What is the right scope for a first text-to-SQL pilot?

Scope small or fail. It matches Genie's own 5-or-fewer-tables guidance and keeps the golden set tractable. Opening the whole warehouse is the top adoption anti-pattern - accuracy craters and trust dies.

2 · What single number should a leader track weekly during a pilot?

A hard accuracy number on real questions, tracked over time, keeps a rollout honest. Silent failures - plausible wrong answers - are tracked separately because they are the dangerous kind.

3 · When is "wait" the right decision?

If nobody can define "revenue" or "active customer" in one sentence, no tool will either. Writing those definitions (the curation tax) is the highest-return first move, and it helps every dashboard, not just the AI tool.
