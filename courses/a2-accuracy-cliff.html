Part 0

Why an executive needs benchmark literacy

Every vendor will show you an accuracy number. None of those numbers are comparable, and most are measured on tests that flatter the product (a3 goes deep on that). The defense is cheap: know the four public benchmarks, know roughly what frontier models score on each, and know which one looks like your warehouse. That is 15 minutes of learning that changes every vendor conversation you will ever have in this category.

Part 1 · covers Spider 2.0 + BIRD headline results

The benchmark ladder in plain English 15 min live

Academia has spent a decade building harder and harder tests for text-to-SQL. Read bottom to top and it is a story of progress; read top to bottom - the direction your warehouse sits in - and it is a cliff.

LiveThe four steps, one line each6 min▶
  • WikiSQL (2017) - solved. 80,654 questions over single Wikipedia tables. Useful history, no longer informative.
  • Spider (2018) - the academic standard. 10,181 questions across 200 multi-table databases. Frontier models score roughly 86-91% execution accuracy. This is the difficulty level every polished vendor demo operates at.
  • BIRD (2023) - realism arrives. Dirty data, external business knowledge required. Humans score 92.96%; GPT-4 scored 54.89%. One telling detail: adding a single sentence of business "evidence" per question lifted GPT-4 by about 20 points - the earliest hard proof that context beats model size.
  • Spider 2.0 (2025) - enterprise reality. 632 problems from real enterprise workflows: databases with 1,000+ columns, answers running past 100 lines of SQL. GPT-4o: 10.1%, versus 86.6% on original Spider. Same model, real work, one-eighth the score.

The board-ready sentence: "The models that ace the benchmark in the demo score about 10% on the benchmark that looks like our warehouse."

Self-studyThe hopeful footnote - agents climb the cliff2 min read▶

Spider 2.0 is not a dead end. By mid-2026 the leaderboard is dominated by agentic systems - pipelines that retrieve metadata, execute, catch errors, and retry - scoring near 96.7 on the Snow setting versus 10-17 for bare models. Read that carefully: the models did not get nine times smarter in a year. The systems around them got better at assembling context and checking work. That is precisely the engineering (and curation) effort a4 teaches you to budget for, and it is why "which model do you use?" is one of the weakest questions you can ask a vendor.

Part 2 · covers the failure-mode framing

Silent vs loud failure: the executive risk concept 10 min live

If you retain one idea from this entire track, make it this one. Text-to-SQL systems fail in two ways, and they are not equally dangerous. A loud failure throws a database error: annoying, visible, honest. A silent failure runs perfectly and returns a plausible wrong number - and plausible wrong numbers travel.

LiveWhy silent failures are a governance problem, not a bug4 min▶

A dashboard built by an analyst carries implicit accountability: someone wrote that query, someone can be asked. A chat answer carries the same visual confidence with none of the accountability - the "author" is a model that cannot be asked what it meant. Three consequences for a leader:

  • Wrong-but-plausible is the #1 risk of the category - not downtime, not cost, not even data leakage (a5 covers those). Budget your skepticism accordingly.
  • Loud failures are recoverable by machines; silent ones are only recoverable by humans who know what the number should roughly be. Your experienced analysts become more valuable, not less.
  • Any tool you evaluate must be measured on silent failures specifically - a3 turns that into a bake-off procedure, a6 into a pilot design.
Real world

The pilot that reported revenue 30% high for days. A BI team piloted a chat-with-data tool. Week one, the demo wowed the sponsor on rehearsed questions. Week three, a regional manager asked for last quarter's revenue and got a number 30% high - the tool had summed list prices instead of order-line prices. The query ran perfectly. Nothing errored. Nobody noticed for days, because the number was plausible. The eventual fix was not a better model: it was writing the definition of revenue somewhere the system could read, and a golden set of checked questions that would have caught the miss in minutes.

Part 3 · covers the error-analysis evidence

Why the cliff exists - and why the fixes are context 8 min live

The cliff is not mysterious, and it is not a model IQ problem - the same model scores 86.6% and 10.1% depending on the test. Two structural causes, one conclusion.

LiveTwo causes, one conclusion5 min▶
  • Cause 1: enterprise schemas are huge and cryptic. Thousand-column databases, tables named by systems long retired, three tables that all look like they hold revenue. Published error analysis of 4,602 real failures found 81.2% were schema errors - the model picked the wrong table or invented a column. Wrong schema pick is also exactly how silent failures are born.
  • Cause 2: business meaning lives in nobody's schema. What counts as "revenue"? Delivered orders only? Net of refunds? Which fiscal calendar? No schema dump answers that - it lives in your analysts' heads, in dbt docs, in tribal knowledge. BIRD proved the point quantitatively: one sentence of business evidence per question was worth ~20 points of accuracy to GPT-4.
  • Conclusion: every fix that works is context engineering. Retrieving the right metadata, supplying example queries, writing metric definitions into a semantic layer, letting the system check and retry. The next session shows how each vendor packages exactly these levers; a4 shows what they cost your team to maintain.
Self-studyThe question to carry into every vendor meeting1 min read▶

Not "how accurate is it?" but "accurate on what?" Any accuracy claim without a named benchmark and a described test set is marketing. And if the benchmark named is Spider-level - or the vendor's own internal set - you now know exactly which step of the staircase you are being shown, and which step you actually live on. a3 sharpens this into a full evaluation checklist.

Watch it fail, live ★ 8 min · no code, just click

Below is a naive text-to-SQL system over a small coffee-business warehouse - the same one the builder track dissects. You are about to see, in three clicks, the demo effect and both failure families. The SQL it writes really executes in your browser.

Ask Q1 (how many customers). It answers correctly - this is the demo effect. Easy questions work, and vendor demos are built from easy questions.

Ask Q4 (total revenue from delivered orders). The query runs cleanly and returns a plausible number - which is wrong: it quietly includes cancelled orders. This is the silent failure from Part 2, live. Read the "Why" note under the result.

Ask Q11 (which channel drives the most revenue). It confidently answers a different question - most orders, not most revenue. Metric ambiguity: the failure a written metric definition exists to prevent (a4).

🤖 What you are looking at The "model" here is a deterministic simulation built for teaching: each question carries the characteristic mistake real LLMs make when the relevant context is missing, at rates the research on this page documents. The SQL genuinely runs against a real SQLite database in your browser - right and wrong are decided by execution, not by us.
Demo 2 of 2

The honest baseline number ★ 4 min · run the scorecard

Three questions is an anecdote. Here is the measurement: all 12 questions of a small golden set, run through the same naive system, scored by comparing each result against a human-verified answer. This is execution accuracy - the same metric Spider and BIRD use, on a set small enough to read in full.

★ The number to remember Around 8% - one question in twelve. That is what raw "point an LLM at the schema" delivers on questions with business meaning, and it rhymes with the published range: ~3% in open-source framework research, ~10% on Spider 2.0, near 0% on the BEAVER enterprise benchmark. When a vendor shows you 90%+, your first question is now automatic: measured on what, and who curated it? That question is the whole of session a3.
Homework

Ask your team - this week ◐ 30 min of conversations

  • Ask your data lead how many tables and columns your main warehouse has. If it is hundreds of tables, you live on the Spider 2.0 step of the staircase - say so in the next vendor meeting.
  • Ask for the last incident where a wrong number reached leadership. How long did it circulate, and what caught it? That is your organization's silent-failure detection time.
  • Ask which three metrics have the most contested definitions ("revenue", "active", "churn" are the usual suspects). Those are where a chat tool will fail silently first.
  • Ask any vendor in your pipeline: "what benchmark is your accuracy number measured on, and can we see the questions?" Note whether the answer names Spider-level academic sets or an internal set.
  • Replay Demo 1 for one colleague - Q1 then Q4 - and watch their reaction to the plausible wrong revenue number. That reaction is the risk conversation this session exists to start.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · On the benchmark ladder, where do vendor demos live?

Demos operate at the difficulty where models shine: clean multi-table questions, ~86-91% territory. Your warehouse looks like Spider 2.0, where the same models score ~10%. "Accurate on what?" is the question that exposes the gap.

2 · What is a silent failure?

Loud failures announce themselves and stop at the user. Silent ones look exactly like right answers - Q4's cancelled-orders revenue in the demo is the canonical case. Danger ranking: silent wrong > loud error > no answer.

3 · In published error analysis of 4,602 real text-to-SQL failures, roughly what share were schema mistakes (wrong table or column)?

81.2% were schema errors. The model writes fluent SQL about the wrong things - which is why the highest-value fixes are about getting the right schema and meaning into context, not about model size.
