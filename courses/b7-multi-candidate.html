Multi-candidate generation: ask the model ten times

Real LLMs are nondeterministic - ask the same question twice, get different SQL. Tonight you learn the production trick that turns that flaw into an asset: generate N candidate queries, execute them all, score or vote, return the best. It is how Salesforce reports climbing from roughly 50% to roughly 80% efficacy, and it needs no new context lever at all - just more shots on goal and a way to pick.

Part 1 · covers Salesforce case, CHASE-SQL

The trick: N shots, one winner 8 min live

Sample the model several times at nonzero temperature and the correct answers tend to converge - different SQL text, same result set - while the mistakes scatter: each wrong candidate tends to be wrong its own way. Execute everything, group by result, and the truth usually forms the biggest cluster. Nondeterminism, the thing demo audiences fear most, becomes the raw material of reliability.

LiveThe production story and the research twin5 min▶
  • Salesforce, in production (vendor-reported). Their text-to-SQL agent launched at roughly 50% efficacy. The fix that took it to roughly 80% was not a bigger model or more metadata - it was generating 10 candidate queries per question and scoring them. Treat the exact numbers with the usual vendor-claim caution; the shape of the move is the lesson.
  • CHASE-SQL, in the research. The academic twin generates diverse candidates by design - its divide-and-conquer strategy decomposes the question different ways so the candidates are not ten near-clones but genuinely different attack angles - then a selection stage picks the winner. Diversity in, reliability out.
  • Why it works. A single sample is a coin flip weighted by your context quality. N samples turn the same weighted coin into a distribution you can read. The stronger your context stack (b2-b5), the more the distribution piles onto the right answer - multi-candidate amplifies good context; it cannot replace it.
Real world

The 50 → 80 memo. Salesforce's engineering write-up on their production agent is unusually candid: launch efficacy around 50%, users unhappy, and the recovery plan was sampling plus scoring rather than waiting for a better model. Ten candidates per question at production scale is a real bill - and they judged it cheaper than wrong answers. That trade, vendor-reported but load-bearing, is tonight's whole session in one anecdote.

The only change to generation - sample N times instead of once System: You are a SQL expert. Given the schema, docs & examples below, write one SQLite query that answers the question. Return only SQL. [ ...same context stack as b2-b5: schema, examples, retrieved docs, semantic definitions... ] Question: What is our total revenue from delivered orders? -- Call the model N=10 times at temperature 0.7-1.0 (not 0!). -- Zero temperature would return 10 identical candidates: -- diversity is the point. Then execute all 10 and vote.
Part 2 · covers self-consistency, execution filtering, LLM-as-judge

Picking the winner: three selection strategies 7 min live

Generating candidates is the easy half. The pattern lives or dies on selection - and the field has settled on three strategies you can stack, ordered here from cheapest to priciest.

LiveThe three strategies, compared4 min▶
StrategySignalCost · caveat
Execution-based filteringDid the query run without a database error?Nearly free (you execute anyway). Catches loud candidates only - b6's limit applies per candidate.
Self-consistency (majority vote)Group candidates by result set; return the biggest cluster's queryFree after execution. Fails when a systematic bias makes the majority wrong the same way - shared missing context lies to the vote.
LLM-as-judge rankingA model scores each candidate against the question and schemaOne more model call per ranking. Judges have taste but no ground truth - calibrate them, which is a whole discipline (learn-evals).

Stack them in that order and most questions never reach the judge. Note the self-consistency caveat carefully: if all ten candidates were generated without the semantic layer, all ten may happily include cancelled orders - unanimous and wrong. The vote measures agreement, not truth. Context quality still decides what the candidates agree on.

Self-studyThe learn-evals bridge2 min read▶

LLM-as-judge is not a text-to-SQL invention - it is the general evaluation pattern from the learn-evals course, pointed at candidate ranking. Everything that course teaches transfers: judges drift, judges favor verbose candidates, judges need their own golden set to be trusted. If you plan to let a judge break ties in production, first measure the judge the way b8 measures the generator. A scoring stage you have never evaluated is just a second opinion from a stranger.

Part 3 · covers the cost math

The cost reality: N of everything 4 min live

Ten candidates means roughly 10x the generation tokens and 10 executions per question - every question, forever. The pattern is a dial, not a default. Where you set it is a business decision about what a wrong answer costs.

LiveWhen N candidates are worth it - and when not3 min▶
  • Worth it: high-stakes numbers that leave the building - board metrics, finance reconciliations, anything quoted in a decision. A wrong revenue figure costs more than 10x tokens ever will. This is where Salesforce spent it.
  • Not worth it: casual exploration, quick-look queries, an analyst iterating who will eyeball the SQL anyway. One candidate plus b6's retry loop is the right spend there.
  • The middle path: adaptive N - one candidate by default, escalate to 5-10 when the question matches high-stakes patterns (your semantic layer already knows which metrics are governed) or when the first result fails a sanity check.
  • Executions are not free either: on a warehouse that bills per query, 10 executions of a heavy scan is real money and real latency. Candidate SQL should hit a limited or sampled target when possible.
Our golden set is saturated - do not read tonight's 100% as the pattern's ceiling On this course's 12 curated questions, the full lever stack already scores 12/12, so multi-candidate has nothing left to add here. Real question sets are never saturated: users invent question 13 daily. Multi-candidate earns its keep exactly on the residual - the questions your context stack half-covers, where one sample flips a coin and ten samples read the distribution.
Demo 1 of 2

Two roads to the same number ★ 12 min · everyone builds

Self-consistency rests on one fact: differently written SQL can be equally correct - same result set, different text. Prove it with your own hands. Golden Q6 asks for average order value; below is the subquery form. Your job: write its CTE twin, run both, and watch two shapes agree on one number. That agreement is exactly the signal a majority vote counts.

Your exercise Rewrite the query using a CTE: WITH order_totals AS (...) then select the rounded average from it. Run both versions. If your CTE twin returns the same value, you have just cast two votes for the same result set - self-consistency in miniature. (Stuck? The CTE body is the inner query, verbatim.) 
SELECT ROUND(AVG(order_total), 2) AS avg_order_value FROM (
  SELECT SUM(quantity * unit_price) AS order_total
  FROM order_items GROUP BY order_id
);

This is also why the field measures execution accuracy by result set, not SQL text (b1): a string-match metric would call your correct CTE twin a failure. Voting inherits the same principle - candidates are grouped by what they return, never by how they are written.

The vote, as a thought experiment ★ 10 min · reason it out

Our simulator is deterministic, so it cannot fan out ten noisy candidates - instead, you play the scorer. Imagine DaybreakGPT sampled four candidates for Q4 (total revenue from delivered orders) and the executed results came back as follows. Work the steps, then verify against the live playground.

The returns: candidate A says 4187.50; candidates B, C, and D all say 3245.75. All four ran without error, so the execution filter keeps everything. Which number do you ship?

Majority vote: group by result set - cluster {B, C, D} has three votes, {A} has one. Self-consistency says 3245.75. But a vote is evidence, not proof - so check the story behind the split.

The delivered-filter check: you know this database. 4187.50 is the sum over all order lines - A forgot the delivered-only rule (b1's classic silent failure). B, C, D carry the WHERE clause in three different SQL shapes. Majority and domain check agree: 3245.75.

Verify live: ask Q4 in the playground below with the full stack on - the gold answer matches the majority cluster. Then flip the semantic lever off and ask again: you just generated candidate A yourself.

★ The takeaway from the thought experiment Notice what made the vote trustworthy: the majority cluster agreed and the minority had an explainable failure story. When all candidates share the same missing context, they can agree unanimously on the wrong number - the vote cannot save you from a context gap, only from sampling noise. Multi-candidate amplifies the stack from b2-b5; it never replaces it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Real LLMs are nondeterministic. Why does multi-candidate generation work anyway?

Nondeterminism becomes the asset: N samples turn a weighted coin flip into a readable distribution. Temperature 0 would defeat the purpose (10 identical candidates carry one candidate's information), and each sample is no better alone - the vote is what improves.

2 · What is the cheapest reliable selection signal?

You execute the candidates anyway, so "ran without error" and "agrees with the most siblings" cost nothing extra. The judge costs another model call - save it for ties. Shipping N raw candidates to a business user defeats the product.

3 · What is the honest cost tradeoff of N-candidate generation?

Parallelism helps latency, not the bill: every candidate is paid for in tokens and in warehouse execution. That is why the pattern is a dial - Salesforce spent it where wrong answers cost more than compute, and adaptive N keeps exploration cheap.
