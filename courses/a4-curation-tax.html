Part 0

Why this session decides your budget

Every vendor demo you saw in session 3 ran on a database someone spent weeks preparing. The preparation is invisible in the demo and absent from the price list, yet it is where most of the money and calendar time goes. We call it the curation tax: the metadata, definitions, examples, and test questions a system needs before its answers can be trusted. This session prices that tax with published evidence, then lets you watch the biggest single line item - the semantic layer - move accuracy live.

Part 2 · covers semantic-layer paired benchmark (arXiv 2604.25149), dbt-llm-sl-bench

The semantic layer: the tax's biggest payoff 10 min live

Of everything below the waterline, one item has the strongest published evidence: writing down what your business words mean. "Revenue" at your company might mean delivered orders only. "Churn" might mean cancelled subscriptions, not cancelled orders. That meaning lives in nobody's schema - a semantic layer is simply the document where it finally gets written down in a form the system can read.

StudyFindingStatus
Paired benchmark, three frontier models (arXiv 2604.25149)+17 to +23 percentage points from adding one 4KB semantic documentPeer-reviewed measurement

Note the honesty grading: the paired benchmark is the solid one, dbt's numbers come from dbt's own (open, runnable) benchmark, and the insurance case is a vendor-reported claim. Session 5 teaches you why that grading habit matters.

Self-studyWhat a semantic layer actually contains2 min read▶
  • Metric definitions: revenue = sum of order-line value on delivered orders only. One sentence, decades of meetings ended.
  • Vocabulary mappings: "active subscription" = cancel date is empty; "churn" = subscriptions cancelled in period, not orders.
  • Approved join paths: which tables connect, and how - so the system never invents a shortcut.
  • Scope boundaries: what the system is allowed to answer, so out-of-scope questions fail loudly instead of guessing.

The document is small - the paired benchmark's version was 4KB, about a page and a half of text. The cost is not typing; it is getting your organization to agree what the words mean. That agreement is the tax, and it pays off everywhere, not just in AI.

Part 3 · covers curation ownership + drift

Who pays the tax - and when it comes due again 6 min live

The tax is paid in data-team hours, and it is not paid once. Three planning facts to carry into your next budget conversation:

LiveThe three budgeting facts4 min▶
  • The data team writes and maintains the definitions. Business stakeholders must agree on meanings, but someone technical has to encode them, test them against the golden set, and keep them true. That is engineering capacity taken from other work - plan it, don't discover it.
  • Drift is the hidden recurring charge. A schema migration renames a column; a new product line changes what "revenue" includes; a curated example query goes stale. Nothing errors - the curation just quietly stops matching reality, and silent failures creep back. Without an owner and a re-run cadence, quality decays on a schedule nobody set.
  • The budget rule: curation is a product, not a project. Fund it like one - a named owner, a weekly quality number (the golden-set score from session 6), and maintenance time in every schema-change ticket. The alternative is a tool that demos at 90% and quietly drifts toward the uncurated baseline.
Real world

The Genie space that got remodeled. A documented Databricks Genie deployment started at 8 of 15 benchmark questions correct - 53%, a coin flip with confidence. The team did not swap models or buy anything: they systematically remodeled the space - curated the table scope, wrote instructions, added trusted example queries - and reached 15 of 15. Same tool, same model, same warehouse. The entire difference was paid in curation hours. That is the tax working exactly as designed - and note it was measured against a benchmark question set they built first.

Demo · the lever moment

Watch the semantic layer earn its keep ★ 12 min · live on screen

Below is the course's teaching simulator over the Daybreak coffee warehouse. Every other lever is already on - schema linking, examples, retrieval - and the semantic layer is the one switch left. You are about to watch the exact failure this session is about, then measure what fixing it is worth.

Ask Q4 (total revenue) with the semantic layer OFF. The answer comes back instantly and looks completely plausible - and it is wrong: it counts cancelled and pending orders. This is the silent failure that ships into decisions.

Toggle Semantic layer ON and ask Q4 again. Now "revenue = delivered orders only" is in the system's context, and the number is right. One written definition was the entire difference.

Try Q9 (March cancellations) and Q11 (best channel) with the lever off, then on - churn and "best" are business words, and the same pattern repeats.

Now run the scorecard below: the full 12-question golden set, first without the semantic layer (58%), then with it (92%). One curated document is the single biggest jump on the whole ladder.

Homework

Five questions for your team this week ◐ 20 min total

  • Ask your BI lead: "Where is our definition of revenue written down today - in a document a system could read, or in people's heads?" The answer prices your semantic-layer gap.
  • Ask which five metrics generate the most rework and cross-team disputes. Those five are the first entries in your semantic layer - the tax with the fastest payback.
  • Ask who would own metric definitions as a product. If no name comes back, you have found the real blocker - it is organizational, not technical.
  • Ask what happens today when a schema changes: who updates the documentation, and how would a text-to-SQL space find out? That answer is your drift exposure.
  • Ask for the last 20 ad-hoc questions the data team answered for executives. That list is your future golden set - session 6 shows you what to do with it.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What does the curation tax actually buy?

Every documented improvement in this session - Genie's 8/15 to 15/15, the +17 to +23 point semantic-layer gain, the 58% → 92% jump you watched live - came from curated context, not a different model. The tax buys context, and context is the product.

2 · The semantic layer "flips failures from silent to loud." Why does a leader care?

In dbt's benchmark the semantic layer raised accuracy AND changed the failure style: out-of-scope questions now error explicitly instead of guessing. For governance and trust, converting the dangerous failure into the visible one is worth as much as the accuracy gain.

3 · Who maintains the definitions, and on what schedule?

No vendor maintains your business meaning. Drift is the hidden recurring charge: a renamed column or a new product line quietly invalidates curation without any error. The budget rule from this session: fund curation as an ongoing product with a named owner and a weekly quality number.
