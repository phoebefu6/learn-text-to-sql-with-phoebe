Part 1 · covers the demo-vs-production gap

The oldest promise in BI 15 min live

Every BI generation has promised the same thing: business people ask their own questions, no analyst queue, no ticket backlog. Each wave got closer, and each wave still ended with the data team writing the queries - because the hard part was never the interface. It was the meaning: which tables, which joins, what "revenue" includes. LLMs finally made the demo of the dream work. Whether the product works is a different question, and the gap between the two is this entire course.

LiveWhy LLMs finally made the demo work5 min▶

Pre-LLM self-serve tools made users learn the tool - drag fields, build filters, know which dataset to open. LLMs removed that: plain English in, SQL out, and on a clean demo database it genuinely works. That is a real advance, not smoke. The trap is what a clean demo hides:

  • Demo databases are small and self-describing. Six well-named tables. Real warehouses have hundreds of cryptically named ones - a2 puts hard numbers on how much that costs.
  • Demo questions avoid business meaning. "How many customers?" needs only the schema. "What was revenue?" needs a definition of revenue that lives in nobody's schema.
  • Demo answers are never checked. A confident wrong number looks identical to a right one - that is a2's silent-failure lesson, the single most important risk idea in this track.
Real world

Salesforce shipped it and published the honest number. Their production text-to-SQL agent launched at roughly 50% efficacy - a coin flip, from a company with world-class engineering, after the demos looked great. Getting to ~80% took generating ten candidate queries per question and scoring them. The lesson for a buyer: even the best builders start far below the demo, and the climb is engineering effort around the model, not a better model.

Self-studyWhy every earlier self-serve wave stalled2 min read▶

Ask a veteran BI lead what happened to the last self-serve push and you will usually hear the same arc: launch enthusiasm, a wave of dashboards, then quiet reversion to the analyst queue. The interface was never the bottleneck. The bottleneck was that answering a business question requires business context - which tables are trustworthy, what filters "count", what the words in the question mean. LLMs changed the interface radically and changed the context problem not at all. That is why this course's thesis is the model is not the product - context is: the winners in this market are the products that manage context well, and the cost you should budget for is context curation (a4 prices it).

Part 2 · covers adoption stats + the category map

Market moment 2026: real adoption, real noise 17 min live

The market is genuinely moving and genuinely noisy at the same time. Two adoption signals worth knowing - both vendor-published, so hold them lightly - and one word that should sharpen your skepticism in every vendor meeting.

LiveThe numbers and the noise6 min▶
  • LangChain's 2026 survey (vendor-published): 57% of organizations report running AI agents in production, and 24.4% name data analysis as the primary agent use case - the single biggest category. If that is even directionally right, chat-with-data is the front line of enterprise AI agents.
  • Gartner (as reported in vendor comparison articles): 40% of enterprise applications will include task-specific agents by end of 2026. Gartner pairs that projection with a warning that matters more than the number: "agent washing" - existing product features relabeled as agents and sold at agent prices.
  • What agent washing sounds like in a meeting: "our AI agent" turns out to be the same autocomplete feature from last year's release. The test question: what does it do differently when it is wrong? A real agentic system checks, retries, or escalates. A relabeled feature just returns the answer with the same confidence either way.

Demo · guided discussion

Score your org: the five-question diagnostic ★ 10 min · everyone answers

No tools, no laptops - just five questions answered honestly, ideally out loud with your data lead in the room. Your answers predict how a text-to-SQL pilot would go in your organization better than any vendor benchmark does.

Do we have metric definitions written down anywhere? One place where "revenue", "active customer", "churn" are defined. If the answer is "it's in people's heads" - that is the first thing any tool will get wrong.

Who answers ad-hoc data questions today, and how long is the queue? This is the pain the dream promises to remove. If you cannot size it, you cannot size the value of fixing it.

What happened to the last self-serve BI push? If it stalled, name why. An LLM interface on top of the same unsolved context problem stalls the same way.

If a chat tool returned a plausible wrong number, how would we find out? If the honest answer is "we might not" - hold that feeling until a2. It is the central risk of this category.

Which 20 real questions would we most want self-served? Write them down. That list becomes your golden set - the measuring stick a6 builds the whole pilot around.

Scoring the diagnostic Confident written answers to 1 and 5 mean you are unusually ready - the curation work (a4) is half done. Blank stares at 1 and 4 mean a pilot today would demo well and fail silently. Neither result says "don't do it"; both tell you what to fix first, and in which session of this track the fix lives.
Homework

Ask your team - this week ◐ 30 min of conversations

  • Ask your BI or data lead: how many ad-hoc data questions arrived last month, and what was the median wait for an answer? That number is the size of the prize.
  • Ask where "revenue" is defined in writing. If three teams give three definitions, you have found your future silent failures before spending a dollar.
  • Ask what happened to the last self-serve BI initiative - launch date, peak usage, and what people quietly went back to doing.
  • Ask any vendor currently in your pipeline which of the four map categories they sit in, and what curation work they expect from your side. Vague answers to the second question are a red flag a4 will explain.
  • Start the 20-question list from the diagnostic with your team. Do not polish it - a rough real list beats a perfect hypothetical one, and a6 will put it to work.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why does every text-to-SQL demo look flawless while production systems struggle?

No trickery needed: the demo environment is genuinely easy. The gap is context - schema scale and business meaning - which is why a2's benchmark cliff exists and why every fix is a context lever.

2 · What is "agent washing"?

The test question in any vendor meeting: what does it do differently when it is wrong? A real agentic system checks, retries, or escalates. A relabeled feature answers with the same confidence either way.

3 · "The model is not the product." What is the product?

Every product in the category map is the same pipeline around some frontier model. What differs - and what your team will spend its time on - is the context layer. a4 prices that work; a3 shows how each vendor packages it.
