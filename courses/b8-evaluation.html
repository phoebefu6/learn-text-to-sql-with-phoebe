Part 1 · covers Spider's EX/EM metric contribution

Metrics properly: execution accuracy vs exact match 8 min live

Spider (2018) gave the field its two canonical metrics. Exact Match (EM) compares the predicted SQL string to the gold SQL string. Execution Accuracy (EX) runs the predicted SQL against the database and compares result sets. In the LLM era only one of them survives - because LLMs write SQL that is different from gold and still correct, constantly.

LiveWhy exact match died - and what EX demands of you4 min▶

Pre-LLM parsers were trained to imitate gold SQL, so string comparison roughly worked. LLMs compose SQL freely: different aliases, different join orders, a subquery where gold used a window function. All correct, all EM failures. That is why every number this course has quoted - Spider's ~86-91%, BIRD's 54.89%, Spider 2.0's 10.1% - is execution accuracy.

  • EX is a systems requirement, not just a metric. To measure it you need a runnable database with representative data. That is why your golden set lives next to a real (or sampled) copy of the warehouse - and why this course put SQLite in your browser.
  • Compare result sets, not vibes. The scorecard below does literally this: run generated SQL, run gold SQL, JSON-compare the results. Same mechanics as the academic harnesses.
  • EX inherits the gold label's flaws. If the gold SQL is wrong, EX faithfully reports correct systems as failures. Hold that thought - it is Part 3.
Self-studyBridge to learn-evals: EX is a task-specific eval2 min read▶

If you took learn-evals, place EX in that map: it is an offline, golden-set, deterministic-grader evaluation. The grader is the database itself - which is the luxury of text-to-SQL. Most LLM tasks need an LLM judge with all its noise; here, execution gives you a free, perfectly consistent judge. The discipline that course teaches still applies: version the set, re-run on every change, track the trend not the single number, and audit the labels (tonight's Part 3 is that lesson with published receipts).

The benchmark ladder as an evaluation lesson 8 min live

The field's four flagship benchmarks form a difficulty ladder, and each rung teaches an evaluation design lesson: what you include in the test set decides what your score means.

LiveWhat each rung teaches an evaluator4 min▶
RungWhat it addedEvaluation lesson
WikiSQL (2017)Scale: 80,654 questions, but single-table WikipediaA solved benchmark measures nothing - retire tests the field has memorized
Spider (2018)Multi-table joins, 200 DBs, unseen schemas at test time; introduced EX and EMGeneralization to new schemas is the real test, not new questions on known schemas
BIRD (2023)Dirty data, 33.4 GB, external knowledge; human 92.96% vs GPT-4 54.89% at the timeOne sentence of "evidence" per question moved GPT-4 +20pp (34.88 to 54.89) - context in the test set changes everything the score means
Spider 2.0 (2025)632 real enterprise workflows, 1,000+ columns, 100+ line answersGPT-4o falls from 86.6% to 10.1% - academic scores do not transfer to your warehouse

Notice the BIRD evidence result: the gap between GPT-4 with and without one human-written hint sentence is nearly identical to the human gap with and without the same hints. Context is the bottleneck for people too - which is the course thesis wearing an evaluation hat.

Part 3 · covers CIDR 2026 UIUC benchmark audit (p5-jin)

The scandal: half the gold labels are wrong 7 min live

In a CIDR 2026 paper, a UIUC team audited the benchmarks the whole field ranks itself on. The finding: 52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev's gold annotations contain errors. Spider 2.0-Snow: 66.1%. The most common error type is a mismatch between the gold query and the actual data or schema - the "correct answer" does not correctly answer the question on the database as it exists.

LiveWhat breaks when gold is wrong4 min▶

Execution accuracy compares your system's results to gold's results. When gold is wrong, a correct system scores as a failure and a system that reproduces gold's mistake scores as a success. Rankings computed on such labels are noise wearing a leaderboard costume.

  • The re-scoring proof: on a corrected subset, the CHESS system jumped from 62% to 81% EX - and from 4th place to 1st. Same system, same predictions; only the labels were fixed.
  • The dominant error family: gold query vs data/schema mismatch - exactly the class of silent failure this course has taught you to fear in your own systems, appearing in the measuring stick itself.
  • The working conclusions: read SOTA claims skeptically; never pick a vendor off a leaderboard delta of a few points; and audit your own gold labels with the same energy you audit your agent.
Real world

CHESS was quietly the best system on BIRD Mini-Dev - and nobody knew. Its team shipped, benchmarked, and sat in 4th place at 62% while systems that happened to echo broken gold labels ranked above it. Only when auditors corrected the labels did its real 81% surface. If a published, peer-scrutinized leaderboard can misrank systems by 19 points, an internal eval nobody has audited certainly can. The audit's companion repo (github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/text_to_sql_benchmarks) documents every corrected label.

Self-studyThe rule that survives: your set, your data, your audit2 min read▶

Public benchmarks answer "how do models compare on academic tasks" - noisily, it turns out. They cannot answer the only question your stakeholders have: "does it work on our warehouse, our vocabulary, our metric definitions?" A 20-question golden set mined from your real BI backlog beats Spider for that purpose on day one. And the audit lesson applies recursively: have a second analyst re-verify each gold query against the live database, on a schedule, because your schema drifts (b10 makes that a production rule).

Your benchmark harness - then break its gold ★ 12 min · everyone builds

The scorecard below is a complete evaluation harness: golden questions, gold SQL, a live database, execution, result-set comparison. With every lever on, DaybreakGPT should hit 12/12. Then we do to it what the auditors did to BIRD.

Run the golden set with all levers on. 12/12 - that is 8% (b1) to 100% (b7), all from context. Note each row's verdict is computed by executing, not asserted.

Now the thought experiment: imagine Q4's gold query forgot the delivered-only filter - gold sums every order line. Your agent, correctly filtering to delivered, would return different rows.

Verdict under broken gold: ✗ silent. Your agent is scored WRONG for being RIGHT - and a naive agent that also forgot the filter would be scored right for being wrong.

That is precisely the E2 gold-vs-data mismatch that afflicts 52.8% of BIRD Mini-Dev. Your harness is only as honest as its labels - so gold gets audited too.

Audit habit Before trusting any golden set - yours or a benchmark's - pick three questions at random, run the gold SQL by hand, and check the result actually answers the English question on this data. Three minutes, catches the dominant error family.
Demo 2 of 2

Prove EX beats EM with your own hands ★ 8 min · live SQL

Write two queries that differ as strings but must return identical results. Exact match would fail the pair; execution accuracy passes it. Run both and compare the result tables - they are the same rows.

SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS n
FROM customers
GROUP BY city
ORDER BY n DESC
LIMIT 1;
SELECT c.city, COUNT(c.customer_id) AS n
FROM customers c
GROUP BY c.city
ORDER BY COUNT(c.customer_id) DESC
LIMIT 1;
★ The point, in one sentence Correctness in this field is a property of results on a database, not of SQL text - which is why a golden set without a database to run against is not an evaluation, just a wish list.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 25-35 min total

  • In the Demo 1 scorecard, turn levers off one at a time and re-run. For each drop, check which questions flipped and whether they failed loud (error) or silent - you are reading an error analysis, the skill behind every failure-taxonomy paper this course cites.
  • Write a "version C" for Demo 2: a third SQL string (try a subquery or a HAVING-free rewrite) that returns the same single row. Every variant is an EM failure and an EX pass.
  • Draft a 10-question golden set for a database you know: mine the last ad-hoc questions your team actually answered, keep the SQL a human verified, and include at least two vocabulary traps ("active", "churn") on purpose.
  • Audit three of your own gold labels the CIDR way: run each gold query, read the result, and ask "does this actually answer the English question on this data?" Log anything suspicious.
  • Optional reading: the CIDR 2026 audit paper (vldb.org/cidrdb/papers/2026/p5-jin.pdf) and its corrected-label repo, github.com/uiuc-kang-lab/text_to_sql_benchmarks.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Why did the field abandon Exact Match in favor of Execution Accuracy?

EM compares strings, so a different alias or join order fails a perfectly correct query. EX executes both and compares result sets - which is also why a golden set must run against a real database, and why EX still inherits any flaw in the gold labels.

2 · What did the CIDR 2026 UIUC audit find in the flagship benchmarks?

Half or more of the gold labels contain errors, dominated by gold queries that do not match the actual data or schema. Corrected labels changed both scores and rankings - so leaderboard positions are unreliable evidence.

3 · Why does your own golden set beat any public leaderboard for choosing or improving a system?

Public benchmarks generalize across domains and, per the audit, carry high label-error rates. A small set mined from your real BI backlog measures the deployment you actually run - provided you audit its gold labels too.
